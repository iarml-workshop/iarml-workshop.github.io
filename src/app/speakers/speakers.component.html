Confirmed Keynote Speaker:

Philippe Langlais
University of Montreal, Canada
From analogical learning on formal analogies to diagnosing lexico-semantic limitations of LLMs

Abstract. In this talk, I will present three types of analogical studies I have been involved in. The first uses analogical learning on formal analogies to solve mapping problems (such as machine translation) and which presents specific algorithmic challenges. The second focusses on training (neural) models to solve (primarily) semantic analogies at the sentence level. The third explores prompting large language models to assess their ability to capture fine-grained lexico-semantic information. Those studies, conducted over the past twenty years, reflect the evolution of Natural Language Processing -- from dedicated algorithms to prompting large language models.

Bio. Philippe Langlais is a full professor at the Computer Science and Operational Research department of University of Montreal, working on Natural Language Processing at RALI. He did his Phd at University of Avignon (on the use of prosody in Speech Recognition). He visited IDIAP/Martigny for some years (collecting a large speech database), KTH/Stockholm (working on Speech Technology for 2nd Language Acquisition), and LIMSI/Paris-Orsay (now LISN). He his interested in many NLP related topics, including core-technology components (e.g. named-entity recognition, information extraction), and specific applications (e.g. Question-Answering, Machine Translation, Legal Prediction).

Francesca Toni
Imperial College London, UK
Computational Argumentation for Explainable Case-Based Predictions

AI is increasingly embedded in decision-making systems, making explainability a critical requirement for their trustworthy deployment—especially when these systems rely on data that may carry biases. In this talk, I will explore how transparent, explainable predictions can be generated from casebases by drawing on computational argumentation. I will present two complementary approaches grounded in established argumentation frameworks and analyse the formal connections between them. In the first approach, based on abstract argumentation, cases in a casebase are treated as pre-constructed arguments that interact through an attack relation. In the second approach, using assumption-based argumentation, reasoning from cases contributes to the construction of general frameworks from which arguments are systematically derived. The talk will also discuss argumentative explanations for predictions produced by these approaches. Finally, I will discuss neuro-symbolic extensions of both approaches, which combine the strengths of symbolic reasoning with the adaptability of neural models.

Francesca Toni is Professor in Computational Logic and Royal Academy of Engineering/JP Morgan Research Chair on Argumentation-based Interactive Explainable AI (XAI) at the Department of Computing, Imperial College London, UK, as well as the founder and leader of the CLArg (Computational Logic and Argumentation) research group and of the Research Centre in XAI . She holds an ERC Advanced grant on Argumentation-based Deep Interactive eXplanations (ADIX). Her research interests lie within the broad area of Explainable AI, at the intersection of Knowledge Representation and Reasoning, Machine Learning, Computational Argumentation, Argument Mining, and Multi-Agent Systems. She is EurAI fellow, IJCAI Trustee, in the Board of Directors for KR Inc., member of the editorial board for Theory and Practice of Logic Programming, and general chair for IJCAI2026.

Dafna Shahaf
Hebrew University of Jerusalem
Analogy-Driven AI: A Neuro-Symbolic Approach

Analogy is a cornerstone of human reasoning, yet remains elusive for current AI systems. In this talk, I present a pipeline view of analogy in the age of large language models, spanning four recent lines of work. Taken together, these works illustrate how analogy can serve as a bridge between the creative flexibility of LLMs and the rigor of symbolic reasoning, pointing toward a neuro-symbolic path for building AI systems that reason, explain, and innovate.

Dafna Shahaf is a Full Professor in Computer Science at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem. Prof. Shahaf’s research uses digital traces of human activity to better under- stand human capacities such as humor and creativity, and to develop computer systems that can support these capacities. She received her PhD from Carnegie Mellon University, and was a postdoctoral fellow at Stanford University and at Microsoft Research. Prof. Sha- haf has won multiple awards, including best research paper awards at KDD 2010 and KDD 2017, an ERC starting grant, IJCAI Early Career Award. a Microsoft Research Fellowship, a Siebel Scholarship, Wolf’s Foundation Krill Award, as well as MIT Tech Review’s “Most thought-provoking paper of the week”.
